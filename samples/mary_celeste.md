# The Mary Celeste: The Ship Found Sailing With No One Aboard
thumb: NOBODY ON BOARD

## The Empty Ship
card: The Empty Ship
visuals: Mary Celeste painting | brigantine 1872 | Atlantic Ocean Azores

> Wide shot of a two masted sailing ship drifting alone on a grey Atlantic sea, torn sails, 1872
On the fourth of December, 1872, in the open Atlantic between the Azores and the coast of Portugal, a small ship was sailing in a very strange way.
> Wide shot of a wooden brigantine with a few sailors on deck, one man at the wheel pointing toward the horizon, 1872
The helmsman of a Canadian ship called the Dei Gratia had spotted her about six miles away. She was moving in an odd, unsteady line, as if no one was steering. He called his captain.
> Wide shot of a grey bearded Canadian sea captain in a dark wool coat looking through a brass telescope on the deck of a ship
Captain David Morehouse raised his telescope. He saw sails, some set and some torn. He saw no one at the wheel. He saw no one on deck at all.
> Wide shot of a sea captain and his first mate talking quietly at the rail of a ship, the strange ship far away behind them
There was something else strange. The Mary Celeste had sailed from New York eight days before the Dei Gratia, bound for Genoa. By now, she should have been far ahead of them.
> Wide shot of three sailors in wool coats rowing a small boat across rough grey waves toward an empty sailing ship
Morehouse sent his first mate, Oliver Deveau, and his second mate, John Wright, across in a small boat. As they came close, they read the name painted on her stern. Mary Celeste.
> Wide shot of a strong dark bearded sailor in a wool coat climbing over the rail of an empty ship, ropes hanging over the side
Deveau climbed aboard and called out. Nobody answered. The ship was empty.
> Wide shot of an empty ship's cabin with a small table, a few scattered things, water on the floor, grey light from a skylight
There was plenty of food and water on board. The sailors' clothes and belongings were still there. Nothing of value seemed to be missing. But the ship's small lifeboat was gone, and so were most of the ship's papers and the captain's navigation instruments.
> Wide shot of an old open logbook on a wooden desk inside a ship's cabin, a quill pen and an oil lamp
The last entry in the log was from the morning of the twenty fifth of November. That was nine days earlier, and about four hundred nautical miles away.
> Wide shot of a lonely sailing ship far out on a wide empty sea under heavy clouds
Ten people had sailed on this ship. A captain, his wife, their two year old daughter, and seven crew. None of them was ever seen again.
> Wide shot of an old brigantine fading into sea mist at dusk
For more than a hundred and fifty years, people have asked the same question. What happened on the Mary Celeste? To find out, we have to start where she started. On a small island in Canada.

## A Ship With a Bad Start
card: A Ship With a Bad Start
visuals: Spencers Island Nova Scotia | wooden shipyard 1860 | Bay of Fundy

> Wide shot of a small wooden shipyard on the shore of a bay, workers building the frame of a sailing ship, pine forest behind, 1860
In late 1860, at a shipyard on Spencers Island in Nova Scotia, workers laid the keel of a new ship. She was built from local timber, and on the eighteenth of May, 1861, she was launched.
> Wide shot of a newly launched wooden sailing ship floating in a calm bay, small crowd of villagers on the shore in 1860s clothes
Her name was not Mary Celeste. She was called Amazon. She was about thirty meters long and weighed around two hundred tons.
> Wide shot of a sick sea captain lying in a narrow bed in a small wooden house by the sea, a worried woman beside him
Her first captain was Robert McLellan. On her very first voyage, he fell ill. He died on the nineteenth of June, 1861, just weeks after the launch.
> Wide shot of a wooden sailing ship in the English Channel colliding with another small ship, white cliffs in the distance
Her next captain, John Parker, had a rough time too. Near Maine, the Amazon ran into fishing gear. Later, in the English Channel, she hit another ship and sank it.
> Wide shot of a sailing ship docked in a sunny Mediterranean harbour with old stone buildings, 1861
But then came calmer years. She carried timber and cargo across the Atlantic and into the Mediterranean. One of her later mates said that nothing unusual happened at all.
> Wide shot of a painter at an easel on a sunny harbour quay painting a portrait of a sailing ship, Marseille, 1861
In November 1861, in the French port of Marseille, a local artist painted her portrait. It is one of the very few pictures we have of the ship as she first looked.
> Wide shot of a calm captain at the wheel of a sailing ship on a bright sea, the crew working the sails, 1865
From 1863 to 1867, she was commanded by William Thompson. Those were her quiet years, the years when the Amazon was just another hardworking ship of the Atlantic.
> Wide shot of a wooden sailing ship thrown onto a rocky shore by a storm, waves crashing, dark sky, 1867
Then, in October 1867, a storm drove her ashore on Cape Breton Island. Her owners gave her up as a wreck.
> Wide shot of a man in a heavy coat walking along a stormy beach toward a broken ship lying on its side, Nova Scotia, 1867
A local man named Alexander McBean bought what was left of her. Within a month, he sold the wreck on.
> Wide shot of a man in a 19th century coat and top hat inspecting the broken hull of a ship lying on a beach
The wreck was sold, and sold again. In 1868, an American sailor named Richard Haines bought her for one thousand seven hundred and fifty dollars, and spent far more fixing her up.
> Wide shot of carpenters painting a new name on the stern of a repaired sailing ship in a New York shipyard, 1868
In December 1868, he registered her in New York under a new name. Mary Celeste.
> Wide shot of a busy 1870s New York harbour with sailing ships, wooden piers and brick warehouses
Haines soon lost the ship to his creditors. She was bought by a group in New York, led by a ship owner named James Winchester.
> Wide shot of workers on scaffolding enlarging a sailing ship in a dry dock, timber and tools everywhere, 1872
In early 1872, Winchester spent ten thousand dollars rebuilding her. She was made longer, wider and deeper, and she got a second deck. The Mary Celeste was ready for a new life. And a new captain.

## Captain Briggs
card: Captain Briggs
visuals: Benjamin Spooner Briggs | Sarah Briggs | New York 1872 harbour

> Wide shot of a bearded American sea captain in his late thirties in a dark navy coat standing on a wharf, calm and serious
Benjamin Spooner Briggs was thirty seven years old. He was born in Wareham, Massachusetts, in 1835. He was an experienced captain, a careful man, and a devout Christian.
> Wide shot of the bearded captain and a young woman in a dark 1870s dress with her hair pinned up, standing in a parlour with a small boy and a baby girl
He had married his cousin, Sarah Cobb, in 1862. They had two children. Their son Arthur was seven. Their daughter Sophia was two.
> Wide shot of the young woman in a dark 1870s dress holding a two year old girl, stepping aboard a sailing ship from a wooden pier
Arthur stayed at home with his grandmother.
> Wide shot of a small wooden organ and a sewing table inside a captain's cabin on a ship, a child's cot in the corner
The captain's cabin was made into a small home for the voyage. It was a long trip for a two year old, but captains' families often sailed together in those days. But Sarah and little Sophia would come along on the voyage.
> Wide shot of the bearded captain shaking hands with a stout ship owner in a top hat on a New York pier, barrels stacked behind them
Briggs was not just the captain. He also bought a share in the ship. Four of the twelve shares were his. This was his ship, and his family's future.
> Wide shot of seven sailors in 1870s wool jumpers and caps standing in a line on the deck of a sailing ship
He chose a crew of seven. The first mate was Albert Richardson. The second mate was Andrew Gilling. The steward and cook was Edward Head, who had just married. The other four were seamen from the Frisian Islands of Germany. Two of them were brothers.
> Wide shot of a young first mate in a dark coat and a younger second mate studying a map together on a ship's deck
Richardson was married to a niece of the owner, James Winchester. Gilling was about twenty five, born in New York to a Danish family. The four German sailors were Volkert and Boz Lorenzen, Arian Martens and Gottlieb Goudschaal. By every account, they were a good, steady crew.
> Wide shot of dock workers rolling wooden barrels up a ramp into the hold of a sailing ship at a New York pier, 1872
The cargo was one thousand seven hundred and one barrels of alcohol, loaded at Pier fifty on the East River. It was going to Genoa, in Italy.
> Wide shot of the bearded captain writing a letter by an oil lamp in a small ship's cabin at night
On the third of November, Briggs wrote to his mother. He said the ship was in beautiful trim, and he hoped for a good passage.
> Wide shot of a sailing ship anchored off a green coastline at dawn, a small town in the distance, 1872
On the fifth of November, the Mary Celeste left the pier, but bad weather made her wait off Staten Island. Two days later, on the seventh, she sailed out of New York and into the Atlantic.
> Wide shot of another sailing ship being loaded with wooden barrels of petroleum at a busy harbour in New Jersey
Nearby, in Hoboken, another ship was loading. It was the Dei Gratia, with a cargo of petroleum. On the fifteenth of November, eight days after the Mary Celeste, she sailed too. Her route would cross the empty ship three weeks later.

## What Deveau Found
card: What Deveau Found
visuals: Azores Santa Maria Island | ship deck hatch | brigantine sails

> Wide shot of the dark bearded first mate in a wool coat walking across an empty wet ship deck, torn sails above
Oliver Deveau was an experienced sailor, and later he gave a careful account of everything he saw. His testimony is the most important evidence we have.
> Wide shot of torn and loose sails flapping on the masts of an empty ship, ropes trailing over the side into the sea
Some of the sails were set, some were loose, and some were missing. Parts of the rigging were damaged, and ropes were trailing over the side into the water.
> Wide shot of an open hatch on a wooden ship deck with its cover lying beside it, grey sky
Two of the hatches, at the front and the back of the ship, were open. Their covers were lying on the deck. The main hatch was closed.
> Wide shot of sailors looking down into a ship's hold with water sloshing between wooden barrels, lantern light
Below, there was water in the hold. About a meter of it. That sounds bad, but for a wooden ship of this size, it was not dangerous. She was still floating well.
> Wide shot of a long thin metal rod lying on the deck of a ship next to an open pump, coiled ropes nearby
On the deck, they found a rod used to measure how much water is inside a ship. It had been dropped and left there.
> Wide shot of a ship's binnacle with a broken glass cover and a compass inside, on an empty deck, misty sea
The compass housing had been knocked out of place, and its glass cover was broken.
> Wide shot of the empty space on a ship's deck where a small lifeboat used to sit, ropes cut, grey sea behind
And the small lifeboat, which had been kept on top of the main hatch, was gone.
> Wide shot of a ship's cabin with a neatly made bed, a small glass bottle of oil standing upright on a table, water on the floor
The cabins were wet, because water had come in through the doors and skylights. But they were mostly in order. A small bottle of sewing machine oil was still standing upright. There were no signs of a fight, and no signs of fire.
> Wide shot of a sheathed old sword lying under a bed in a dim ship's cabin
In the captain's cabin, there was a sword in its sheath under the bed. It would later cause a lot of trouble.
> Wide shot of a sea chart on a wooden table with a small island marked, an oil lamp and dividers
The last log entry was written at eight in the morning on the twenty fifth of November. It placed the ship near Santa Maria, the most southern island of the Azores.
> Wide shot of a green volcanic island on the horizon seen from the deck of a sailing ship at morning
If the crew had left that morning, they could have seen land. Santa Maria was close by. And after they left, the empty Mary Celeste kept sailing on her own for about four hundred nautical miles.
> Wide shot of three sailors on the deck of the empty ship hauling ropes and trimming sails, the other ship in the distance
Morehouse made a decision. He would split his crew and sail both ships to Gibraltar, about six hundred nautical miles away. If they made it, they could claim a reward for saving the ship and her cargo.
> Wide shot of two sailing ships far apart on a stormy sea, one with only three sailors on deck fighting the wind
It was a risky plan. Deveau and just two seamen sailed the Mary Celeste, a ship that had left New York with eight men. Morehouse sailed the Dei Gratia with the rest. Both ships were badly short of hands, and they sailed through rough December weather.

## Murder in Gibraltar?
card: Murder in Gibraltar?
visuals: Gibraltar 1872 | Rock of Gibraltar | Vice Admiralty court

> Wide shot of the huge Rock of Gibraltar rising above a harbour with sailing ships, 1872
The Dei Gratia reached Gibraltar on the twelfth of December. The Mary Celeste came in the next morning, after sailing through fog.
> Wide shot of a stern British official with white side whiskers in a black frock coat standing on a dock, studying an empty ship
The British court there took control of the ship. The man in charge of the case was the Attorney General of Gibraltar, Frederick Solly Flood. And he did not believe the sailors' story.
> Wide shot of a surveyor in a bowler hat and a diver in an old canvas diving suit examining the hull of a ship from a small boat
A surveyor named John Austin and a diver named Ricardo Portunato examined the ship. They found no damage from a collision or from running aground.
> Wide shot of a Victorian judge in a wig sitting at a high wooden bench in a small courtroom, clerks writing below
The hearings began on the seventeenth of December, before the Chief Justice of Gibraltar, Sir James Cochrane. Around the world, newspapers followed the case. On the twenty first of December, a New York shipping paper already suggested foul play, perhaps linked to the alcohol.
> Wide shot of a man in a frock coat pointing at long cut marks on the wooden bow of a ship, harbour behind
But they did find long cuts on the bow. Austin also reported possible blood on the sword from the captain's cabin. To Solly Flood, it all pointed to one thing.
> Wide shot of Royal Navy officers in 1870s uniforms inspecting the bow of a ship with lanterns, Gibraltar harbour at dusk
Officers of the Royal Navy came to look as well. They agreed that the cuts had been made on purpose, perhaps with an axe.
> Wide shot of a dark ship's deck at night where shadowy sailors gather around barrels, one holding a lantern
His theory was murder. The crew, he said, had got drunk on the alcohol in the cargo. They had killed the captain, his wife, the child and the first mate, then escaped in the lifeboat.
> Wide shot of a scientist in a Victorian laboratory examining a sword under a microscope, glass bottles on shelves
But the evidence did not hold up. The cargo was industrial alcohol, not something anyone could drink. And when scientists tested the stains on the sword, they found that they were not blood.
> Wide shot of an American naval officer in uniform examining the bow of a ship with a notebook, harbour behind
An American naval officer, Captain Shufeldt, also inspected the ship. He decided the cuts on the bow had been made naturally, by the action of the sea.
> Wide shot of an American consul in a black frock coat reading a report at a desk with an American flag behind him
Shufeldt had been asked to look by the American consul in Gibraltar, Horatio Sprague, who was worried about how the case was going. His report gave the court a very different picture.
> Wide shot of a stout ship owner in a top hat speaking in a wood panelled Victorian courtroom, judges at the bench
The owner, James Winchester, came to Gibraltar and spoke for his captain. He said Briggs was an honest man of the highest character.
> Wide shot of the stern British official writing a long report at a desk by a tall window, the Rock of Gibraltar outside
Solly Flood did not give up. He demanded fifteen thousand dollars from Winchester before he would let the ship go. On the twenty second of January, 1873, he sent his murder theory to the Board of Trade in London. But he had no bodies, no witnesses and no blood.
> Wide shot of a grand Victorian courtroom with a judge in a wig and red robe, papers on the bench
Solly Flood never found proof for his theory. On the twenty fifth of February, 1873, the ship was released. In April, the court gave the salvage reward to the crew of the Dei Gratia. One thousand seven hundred pounds. About one fifth of the value of the ship and cargo.
> Wide shot of sailors on the Dei Gratia looking unhappy at a small purse of money on a table, 1873
Many people felt that was very low. Some historians think the court was hinting that it still did not fully trust the men who found her.
> Wide shot of a sailing ship leaving Gibraltar harbour with a new crew, the great Rock behind, spring 1873
In March 1873, a new crew under Captain George Blatchford finally sailed the Mary Celeste to Genoa, to deliver her cargo. When the barrels were unloaded, nine of them were found empty. It was a small detail, but later it would become a very important clue.

## The Unlucky Ship
card: The Unlucky Ship
visuals: Saint Helena island | Port-au-Prince 1885 | Boston courthouse 1885

> Wide shot of an old sailing ship tied up at a quiet New York wharf, rain, no sailors on board, 1873
The Mary Celeste came back to New York in September 1873. Her owner had hoped to move on. But her story had followed her home.
> Wide shot of a ship owner in a top hat signing papers in a small shipping office, an old sailing ship framed in the window
Few sailors wanted to sail on her. In February 1874, Winchester sold her at a loss. Over the next years she changed hands again and again, working hard on poor routes for little money.
> Wide shot of a lonely island with steep cliffs in the South Atlantic, a small sailing ship anchored below, 1879
In February 1879, her captain, Edgar Tuthill, fell ill and died at the island of Saint Helena. He was the third of her captains to die before his time, after Robert McLellan and Benjamin Briggs.
> Wide shot of a sly looking sea captain with a thin beard whispering with two merchants in a Boston warehouse, 1884
In 1884, a captain named Gilman Parker took command. He and a group of Boston shippers made a plan. They loaded her with cargo that was mostly worthless, wrote it down as valuable, and insured it for thirty thousand dollars.
> Wide shot of a sailing ship deliberately run onto a coral reef near a tropical island, crew watching from a boat, 1885
On the third of January, 1885, near Haiti, Parker ran the Mary Celeste onto a reef called the Rochelois Bank, on purpose. Then he sold what was left of her to the American consul for five hundred dollars.
> Wide shot of a crowded Victorian courtroom in Boston with a jury, a captain in the dock, lawyers in black coats
The insurers became suspicious. In July 1885, Parker and the shippers were put on trial in Boston for fraud. The jury could not agree. The men paid back the insurance money and were set free. Parker died in poverty about three months later.
> Wide shot of a broken ship wreck lying on a reef at sunset, waves washing over it, gulls circling
So the Mary Celeste ended her life as part of a crime. Her wreck was left to the sea on a reef in the Caribbean.

## How a Story Became a Legend
card: How a Story Became a Legend
visuals: Arthur Conan Doyle | Cornhill Magazine 1884 | Victorian newspaper

> Wide shot of a sailing ship rotting at an old wooden wharf, gulls on the masts, grey city behind
The Mary Celeste went back into service, but sailors did not want her. People said she was unlucky. She was sold again and again, at a loss.
> Wide shot of a Victorian newspaper office with men at desks and stacks of printed papers, gas lamps
Then the newspapers and the writers got hold of her. In 1883, a newspaper in Los Angeles printed a version with a meal left untouched on the table. That detail was invented.
> Wide shot of a young Victorian writer with a moustache writing at a desk by a window, stacks of magazines beside him
In January 1884, a young doctor and writer named Arthur Conan Doyle published a short story about the ship. He called her the Marie Celeste. He changed the captain's name, the year and the route, and he added a villain.
> Wide shot of Victorian people reading magazines in a cosy reading room, lamps glowing
It was fiction, but many readers took it as true. Even today, many people still call the ship the Marie Celeste.
> Wide shot of a giant octopus tentacle rising out of a dark sea near a small sailing ship, storybook style, moonlight
After that, the stories grew wilder. In 1904, a magazine suggested a giant octopus. Another claimed there were live chickens on board.
> Wide shot of a Victorian magazine page with an illustration of a ghost ship drifting near tropical islands
In 1906, another magazine moved the whole story more than a thousand nautical miles south, near the Cape Verde Islands. Each new version made the mystery bigger, and made the facts smaller.
> Wide shot of an old sailor telling a story to a group of listeners in a smoky tavern, 1920s
In the 1910s and 1920s, stories appeared from so called survivors. They were full of mistakes, and none of them could be true. All ten people were already lost.
> Wide shot of a 1920s writer typing at a desk covered in old papers, a model sailing ship on the shelf
One of the most famous was a hoax from the 1920s, about a cook called John Pemberton. Many readers believed it. Later, it was shown to be invented. In 1925, another writer accused Captain Briggs himself of murder. He later took it back and apologised.
> Wide shot of a captain on one ship watching another ship through a telescope at night, suspicious mood
Some even suspected the men of the Dei Gratia. Perhaps, the theory went, they had planned it all with Briggs to win the reward. But the Dei Gratia sailed eight days later and was a slower ship. And there was never any evidence of a plan.
> Wide shot of a historian reading old letters at a library desk, piles of books and a model ship beside him
The historian Brian Hicks, who studied the case closely, rejected the idea of a secret plan between the two captains. Briggs owned part of the Mary Celeste. Throwing her away made no sense for him.
> Wide shot of a 1930s cinema with a crowd outside and a big painted poster of a ghost ship
In 1935, a British film called The Mystery of the Mary Celeste brought the legend to cinemas. By then, the real ship had been gone for fifty years. Only the legend was sailing on.
> Wide shot of an old sea chart with a triangle drawn on it, a compass and a magnifying glass on top
Some later writers even blamed the Bermuda Triangle, sea monsters, or aliens. But the Mary Celeste was nowhere near the Bermuda Triangle. And there is no need for monsters. The real evidence tells a stranger and sadder story.

## What Really Happened?
card: What Really Happened?
visuals: ship hold barrels | Atlantic storm | Haiti reef

> Wide shot of a ship's dark hold filled with rows of wooden barrels, a single lantern, mist in the air
So what really happened? We will never know for sure. But the evidence lets us rule some things out.
> Wide shot of a pirate ship with black sails in the distance behind an untouched sailing ship, calm sea
It was not pirates. Pirates would have taken the valuables, and the valuables were still on board.
> Wide shot of a giant squid tentacle rising near a ship, then fading into mist, storybook style
It was not a sea monster. A giant squid could not have taken a lifeboat, the ship's papers and the captain's instruments. Those were carried away by people, on purpose.
> Wide shot of a peaceful empty ship's deck with neatly stored tools and coiled ropes, morning light
It was probably not a mutiny either. There was no sign of a fight, the stains were not blood, and the crew had no way to sell a ship or a cargo of alcohol without being caught.
> Wide shot of the bearded captain and his first mate looking worried, standing by an open hatch, mist rising from the hold
Most experts believe the crew left on purpose, in a hurry, and in fear. And the open hatches give us a clue. When the cargo was unloaded in Genoa, nine of the barrels were empty.
> Wide shot of faint vapour rising from barrels in a dark ship's hold, a lantern glowing, eerie mood
One idea is that alcohol fumes built up in the hold. A small burst of flame or a loud rumble could have made Briggs believe the ship was about to explode. In 2006, a chemist at University College London, Andrea Sella, tested this idea. He built a model of the hold, with paper cartons standing in for the barrels, and filled it with gas. When he lit it, there was a loud blast and a ball of flame. But afterwards, the paper cartons were not even scorched.
> Wide shot of a Victorian sea captain staring in alarm at a sudden blue flash of flame rising from an open hatch at night
A flash like that would have terrified anyone standing on deck. And it would have left no trace for Deveau to find. Briggs's own cousin, Oliver Cobb, was one of the strongest believers in this idea.
> Wide shot of a sailor dropping a sounding rod into a hole in the deck while the captain watches anxiously, stormy sky
Another idea comes from Deveau himself. He thought someone measured the water in the ship and got a false reading. If Briggs believed his ship was sinking fast, he would have ordered everyone into the lifeboat at once.
> Wide shot of a waterspout spinning across a dark sea toward a small sailing ship, dramatic light
Deveau also thought a waterspout might have passed over the ship, pushing water into her and shaking her badly. Others have suggested a small undersea earthquake near the Azores, which can make a ship tremble and roar as if she is breaking apart.
> Wide shot of sailors kneeling around a disassembled wooden ship's pump on deck, tools around them, water at their feet, stormy sky
In 2007, a documentary called The True Story of the Mary Celeste looked again at the evidence. One pump had been found taken apart on the deck. So the crew may have been trying to repair it while water was coming in.
> Wide shot of an old ship's chronometer clock in a wooden box on a chart table, a worried captain beside it
The filmmakers also suggested that the ship's chronometer, the clock used to work out a ship's position, might have been wrong. If so, Briggs may have believed he was close to Santa Maria when he was really about a hundred and twenty miles farther west.
> Wide shot of a small lifeboat on a huge empty sea with no land in sight, grey clouds
If that is true, the lifeboat set off toward an island that was much farther away than anyone on board believed.
> Wide shot of a small lifeboat crowded with people pulling away from a sailing ship, a green island in the distance
Both ideas end the same way. The captain, a careful man with his wife and his little daughter aboard, decides not to take the risk. Land is close. Santa Maria is in sight. They climb into the small boat.
> Wide shot of a sailing ship catching the wind and sailing away from a tiny lifeboat on a vast grey sea
And then the wind fills the sails. The Mary Celeste moves away, faster than they can row. Oliver Cobb believed Briggs tied the lifeboat to the ship with a long rope, ready to climb back on board once the danger passed. In the rush, perhaps that rope was not tied well, or perhaps it snapped. Either way, the little boat is left alone on the open ocean, and the empty ship sails on without them.
> Wide shot of divers in modern gear examining old timber pieces on a sandy reef, turquoise water
And her wreck? In 2001, an expedition led by the writer Clive Cussler announced that it had found her wreck on that reef. But tests on the tree rings in the wood showed the timber came from trees still growing in 1894. That was not the Mary Celeste.
> Wide shot of a deserted motor ship drifting on a tropical blue sea, 1955
The Mary Celeste is not the only ship ever found empty.
The naval historian Rupert Gould listed several other ships that had been found deserted in the Atlantic between 1840 and 1855. In 1955, a boat called the Joyita was found drifting in the South Pacific. Twenty five people were missing. But no story has haunted the sea like hers.
> Wide shot of an empty sailing ship drifting on a calm sea at sunset, sails glowing gold
So the ship is lost, and the ten people who sailed on her are lost too. What remains is a log book, a court record, and the careful words of Oliver Deveau.
> Wide shot of a lonely small lifeboat on a vast calm sea at dawn, mist on the water
The truth may be simple. A frightened captain, a moment of doubt, and a decision made in seconds to protect his family. But the sea kept that secret. And it has never given it back.
